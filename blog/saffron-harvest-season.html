The saffron harvest calendar. When it is picked and why the year matters

Every tin of saffron sold in any month of the year comes from a window of two to four weeks the previous autumn. The harvest is short, the rest of the year is storage. Understanding this is the most useful thing a buyer can do.

Quick answer.

Saffron is harvested in autumn, typically late October through mid-November. The exact window varies slightly by region but is short everywhere: Greece, Spain, Kashmir, Iran, Afghanistan, and Morocco all gather their annual saffron supply within roughly the same three-to-four-week window each fall.

Saffron is a one-harvest-per-year crop. Across every major producing region, including Afghanistan, Iran, Kashmir, Spain, Morocco, and Greece, the entire annual supply of the world's most expensive spice is gathered in a window of roughly two to four weeks each autumn. Everything sold in jars and tins for the rest of the year comes from that short harvest. This is a useful thing to understand when you buy saffron, because it means the freshness of what you are holding is a function of which autumn it was picked, not when it was packed or when it arrived on a shelf.

When the Crocus Blooms

The saffron crocus, Crocus sativus, flowers in autumn rather than spring. The bulbs sit dormant through summer, the plants push up leaves and flower buds as temperatures cool, and the purple flowers open in the morning across a window that varies slightly by region and weather year to year. In Herat Province, Afghanistan, which is the source region for Azaad, first bloom typically arrives in late October and the main harvest runs through November. Reporting from the major Iranian growing region of North Khorasan describes a similar pattern, with harvest beginning in late October and continuing until late November. In Kashmir the picking is usually concentrated in late October and early November. Spanish La Mancha harvest is also October to early November. The picture across regions is consistent: a window of three to four weeks, ending before December.

The flowers must be picked the morning they open. The petals are delicate and the three crimson stigmas inside lose quality once the flower has been exposed to sun and warmth for more than a few hours. Pickers are typically in the fields before sunrise. Accounts from Afghan and Iranian producers describe work starting at four in the morning, so that flowers can be collected and brought to processing before the day warms up. This is one of the reasons saffron remains a hand-harvested crop with no mechanical alternative. Timing matters at the scale of hours.

From Flower to Tin

Once the flowers reach the processing area, the three stigmas are separated from each flower by hand, a process called threading or stripping, and then dried. The drying step is where a great deal of the final quality is determined. Done well, with controlled low heat, the stigmas retain a high proportion of their volatile aroma compound, safranal, and develop the deep crimson colour that defines premium grade. Done poorly, with overheating or excessive time, safranal evaporates and the threads end up paler and less aromatic. The wet-to-dry weight ratio is significant: fresh stigmas lose roughly four-fifths of their weight in drying. A kilogram of finished saffron represents roughly five kilograms of fresh stigmas, picked from somewhere between 150,000 and 200,000 individual flowers, depending on flower size and stigma yield.

After drying, batches are graded, tested in a laboratory for the three ISO 3632 parameters (crocin for colour strength, picrocrocin for taste, safranal for aroma), and packaged. From bloom to packaged tin, the typical timeline at a well-run cooperative is a matter of weeks, not months. Tins on the market in spring or early summer of the following year are usually being filled from the previous autumn's harvest.

The most important date on a tin of saffron is not the packaging date or the best-by date. It is the harvest year. Everything else follows from it.

Why the Harvest Year Matters

Saffron does not improve with age. From the moment the threads finish drying, the volatile aroma compound safranal begins to slowly decline. Crocin, the pigment responsible for the colour, is more stable but also degrades over time, particularly under exposure to light and oxygen. Stored correctly in a sealed, opaque container in a cool dark place, Grade I saffron retains quality for three to four years. Stored poorly, in a clear jar by the stove for example, the same threads can lose a substantial portion of their aroma within a single year.

This is why the harvest year on a tin is more informative than a packaging date. Saffron packaged in May from an October harvest is fresh. Saffron packaged in May using leftover material from an autumn two years prior is not, even though both might display the same packaging date. A genuine producer or importer working with traceable lots can tell you the exact harvest year of the material in the tin you are holding. If a seller cannot tell you which autumn the saffron in front of you was picked, that is a useful signal on its own.

The Regional Harvest Windows

For buyers and cooks who want to understand what they are looking at, the major producing regions and their typical harvest windows are:

  • Afghanistan (Herat Province): late October through late November, with the main concentration in early to mid-November.
  • Iran (Khorasan provinces): late October through late November, similar to Afghanistan, with North Khorasan slightly later than South Khorasan.
  • Kashmir, India (Pampore): late October through early November, the shortest window of the major regions.
  • Spain (La Mancha): late October through early November.
  • Morocco (Taliouine): late October through mid-November.
  • Greece (Kozani): late October through mid-November, with the official PDO harvest window concentrated in the same period as the other major regions.

The variation between regions is small because the crocus responds to similar climatic triggers everywhere it grows: cooling temperatures, the right amount of moisture in the soil after the dry summer, and shortening day length. The plant is remarkably uniform in its calendar across latitudes.

Why Afghan Saffron's Calendar Matters for Herat

Herat Province in western Afghanistan has emerged over the past two decades as a producer of consistently high-grade saffron, frequently testing above the Grade I minimum thresholds set by ISO 3632. Several factors contribute, but the climate of the growing region is central: hot, dry summers that allow corms to fully dry and rest, cool autumns that bring on the bloom uniformly, and very low humidity during the harvest window that aids the drying step. The November harvest in Herat is consistently dry, which is an advantage compared to regions where autumn rain can complicate both picking and drying.

For a buyer of Afghan saffron specifically, the practical implication is that a tin labelled with a harvest year of, say, 2025, corresponds to material picked in roughly the first three weeks of November 2025, threaded and dried over the following days, and tested before packaging. The chain from field to lab is short, and the chain from lab to verified tin is documented. A lot number on the tin traces back to that specific harvest batch and the laboratory analysis of it.

What to Look For When Buying

The harvest year is rarely the most prominent marking on a saffron package, which is itself informative. Producers who are confident in the freshness of their material tend to state the harvest year openly, either on the package or in associated documentation. Producers who are less confident, or who are blending material from multiple years, tend to leave it off. A package that lists only a packaging date, a best-by date, or a country of origin without a harvest year is missing the most relevant piece of information.

The other useful piece of information is the laboratory analysis. A genuine producer of Grade I saffron will have a recent certificate of analysis for the lot in front of you, showing the crocin, picrocrocin, and safranal readings from a laboratory test of that specific batch. Those readings are not estimates and they are not generic. They are specific to the harvest the saffron came from. Combined with a harvest year, they give you a complete and verifiable picture of what you are buying.

The Practical Takeaway

Saffron is unusual among kitchen ingredients in that the entire annual world supply is gathered in a few weeks each autumn. Once you understand this, several things make more sense: why the harvest year is the most important date on a tin, why prices and availability shift seasonally in the trade, and why the question to ask a seller is not "when did you package this" but "which autumn was this picked."

For the cook this changes very little day to day. Saffron stored correctly will deliver its colour, aroma, and flavour for several years after harvest. But for the buyer making a choice between two options on a shelf, the harvest year is the single most useful piece of information after the laboratory grade. A current-harvest Grade I tin is a different product from a Grade I tin filled with material from three autumns ago, even if both technically passed the same laboratory thresholds at some point. The freshness shows in the colour of the bloom, the strength of the aroma, and the depth of the flavour the threads give to a dish.
