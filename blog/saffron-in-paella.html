Cooking With Saffron

Saffron in paella. How much, which grade, and why Bomba rice matters

Paella is one of the few dishes where saffron is not optional. The right quantity per pan, why blooming is non-negotiable, why Spanish tradition uses the rice it uses, and what to avoid when shopping for the saffron itself.

Paella is one of the few dishes in the world where saffron is not an option. The colour, the aroma, the savoury depth that distinguishes a proper paella from a generic rice-and-seafood pan are all functions of the saffron infused into the cooking liquid before the rice goes in. Get the saffron right and the rest of the dish has somewhere to build from. Get it wrong — with too little, with low-grade material, or with safflower posing as saffron — and the dish never quite arrives where it is meant to go.

This is a guide to the saffron question specifically: how much to use, what grade to use, when to add it, and why the Spanish tradition uses what it uses.

How Much Saffron for a Paella

For a paella serving four people — typically around 400 grams of dry rice, usually a short-grain Spanish variety like Bomba or Calasparra — a working amount of saffron is between 30 and 50 threads. That corresponds to roughly a generous pinch, or about 0.1 to 0.15 grams by weight if you are measuring precisely. At Grade I crocin levels above 250, this is enough to produce the deep golden colour and unmistakable aroma that define a proper paella. Using less and the dish reads as pale and indistinct. Using significantly more pushes the bitterness from picrocrocin above the threshold where it stops being a pleasant depth and starts being noticeable as bitterness on the palate.

Scaling for a paella for eight, double the saffron. For a paella for two, halve it. The relationship between saffron quantity and serving size is fairly linear within the normal range.

One thing worth noting: these quantities assume Grade I saffron with verified crocin content. Lower-grade saffron with weaker readings — common in supermarket packets that do not carry an ISO 3632 grade — will require more threads to achieve the same effect, sometimes substantially more. This is one of the practical reasons that the upfront cost difference between quality grades is smaller than it appears. The cook ends up using fewer threads of good saffron than threads of mediocre saffron for the same outcome.

Which Grade and Which Origin

Spanish tradition uses Spanish saffron — almost always La Mancha saffron, the variety grown in the central plains of Spain and carrying its own Protected Designation of Origin. La Mancha saffron is high quality and a defensible choice for paella made anywhere. But the underlying question for the cook is not strictly national. It is whether the saffron in your hand is Grade I by ISO 3632 standards, with verified crocin content, and freshly harvested. The ISO 3632 grade is what determines how the saffron will perform in the pan. The origin tells you about the producer's tradition; the grade tells you what is actually in the threads.

Afghan saffron from Herat Province, Iranian saffron from Khorasan, and high-grade La Mancha saffron all routinely test in the Grade I range and produce excellent paella. The thread itself does not know which country it came from. It only knows whether the harvest, drying, and storage were handled correctly. What matters in the pan is the crocin reading, the freshness, and the absence of adulteration.

The saffron makes the paella. Spanish tradition is right about this. What Spanish tradition is also right about is using the freshest, highest-grade material you can find — whatever country it was picked in.

When to Add the Saffron

The biggest mistake in paella cookery is dropping dry saffron threads into the pan at the same time as the rice. Saffron added dry to a hot pan releases only a fraction of its colour and aroma before being absorbed into starchy rice that gives nothing back. The threads end up partly cooked, partly buried, and the dish ends up under-flavoured even though you have used the correct quantity.

The traditional Spanish approach is to bloom the saffron first. Heat a small amount of the cooking stock or warm water to between 65 and 75 degrees Celsius — hot enough to extract the colour and aroma compounds, not hot enough to drive the volatile safranal off — and steep the threads for ten to fifteen minutes. The liquid will turn deep golden. The threads themselves will look pale and exhausted when the bloom is complete, which is the correct visual signal that the extraction has worked. The full bloom — threads and golden liquid together — then goes into the paella stock before the rice. Both the colour and the aroma are now distributed evenly through the liquid, and the rice will absorb them as it cooks.

An alternative used by some cooks is to lightly toast the saffron threads in a dry pan for five to ten seconds before grinding them with a mortar and pestle, then adding the powder to warm stock. The toasting concentrates the aroma compounds and the grinding increases the surface area for extraction. This is faster than blooming and produces a stronger colour, but it requires care: a few seconds too long in the pan and the threads burn, taking the aroma with them. The wet-bloom approach is more forgiving and the result is essentially equivalent.

Why Bomba Rice Specifically

Paella is a rice dish, and the rice variety matters. Bomba is a short-grain rice grown in Spain that absorbs roughly three times its volume in liquid without releasing the starch that would turn the dish creamy. The grains stay separate and firm. This is what allows a paella to develop the prized socarrat — the thin crisp layer of rice on the bottom of the pan — without becoming a risotto or a porridge.

The connection to saffron is simple: a rice that absorbs three times its volume in liquid carries three times the saffron-infused stock per gram. The golden colour penetrates the grain itself rather than just coating it. The aroma travels with the liquid into the centre of each grain. Bomba is the variety the tradition selected for, in part, because it carries saffron well. Calasparra rice, also Spanish, works similarly. Long-grain rice does not. A paella made with long-grain rice is missing one of the structural elements that makes the saffron register the way it should.

The Liquid Question

Paella is traditionally cooked in a wide, shallow steel pan with the rice spread in an even layer roughly two centimetres deep. The ratio of liquid to rice in classic Bomba paella is around three to one by volume — three cups of stock for one cup of dry rice. The stock is added all at once after the sofrito and protein are seared, then the rice is scattered evenly across the pan, and from that point the dish is not stirred. The grains absorb the stock in place.

This is why blooming the saffron in advance and adding the full bloom to the stock is the right approach. Once the rice is in, the saffron has no second chance to distribute itself. Whatever is in the liquid at the moment the rice goes in is what will end up in the finished dish. A liquid that has been properly infused with bloomed Grade I saffron carries the golden colour, the floral-bitter aroma, and the savoury depth straight into every grain.

What Goes With the Saffron

The other defining ingredient of paella is pimentón — Spanish smoked paprika — added to the sofrito early in the cooking. Pimentón and saffron pair well: the smoky depth of the paprika provides a base that the floral lift of saffron sits on top of. A small quantity of sweet paprika alongside the saffron is traditional in Valencian-style paellas. The savoury base of garlic, tomato, and onion in the sofrito is built before the stock goes in, and the saffron-infused stock is poured over the seared base. This sequence — sofrito, protein, stock with bloomed saffron, rice — is the structural backbone of classical paella.

A small amount of fresh rosemary, added to the pan during the final stage of cooking, is common in interior paellas from the Valencian region. The rosemary's pine-resin quality cuts the richness without competing with the saffron. Lemon wedges at the table provide acid that brightens the saffron's floral notes. Beyond these, paella is famously a dish that accepts many proteins — rabbit, chicken, snails, prawns, mussels, squid, depending on region and season — but the saffron, the rice, and the pan are constant across all of them.

The Adulteration Risk

Paella has historically been a target for saffron fraud, particularly in tourist-facing restaurants and supermarket "paella spice mixes." The standard adulteration is to substitute or supplement saffron with safflower petals — a much cheaper product that produces a yellow colour in water but no aroma and no flavour. A paella coloured with safflower looks the part on the plate but tastes flat. The two are easy to tell apart if you know what to look for: saffron threads are crimson with a slight orange tip, safflower petals are uniform yellow-orange and flat rather than filamentous.

The other common substitute is turmeric, which produces a yellow colour and has its own distinct earthy aroma — pleasant in many dishes, but not paella. A paella that tastes faintly of turmeric was coloured with turmeric, not saffron. The two ingredients have nothing in common other than the colour they impart, and the difference is immediately apparent on the palate.

The Practical Recipe

For a paella for four — four hundred grams of Bomba rice, twelve hundred millilitres of stock — the saffron protocol is straightforward. Take thirty-five to forty threads of Grade I saffron with a verified crocin reading. Warm fifty millilitres of the stock to around seventy degrees Celsius in a small saucepan. Add the threads, stir gently, and leave them to steep for twelve to fifteen minutes while the sofrito and protein are prepared. The liquid will turn deep golden. When the rice goes into the pan, pour the bloomed saffron — liquid and threads together — into the rest of the stock and add the combined liquid to the pan. Spread the rice evenly. Do not stir. The saffron will distribute itself through the absorption of the stock, and the colour, aroma, and depth will arrive in the finished dish without further intervention.

This is the operation Spanish tradition arrived at over centuries of trial and error. Get the saffron quality right, get the bloom right, and the rest of the paella follows.
